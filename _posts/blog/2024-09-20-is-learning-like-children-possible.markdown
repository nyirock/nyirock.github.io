I have recently read the Learn Like a Child section from The Writer's Way (Rawlins & Metzger, 2016). While there are undoubtedly some inspirational ideas to be taken up from the section, the overall impression comes off as one-sided and its weakest point stems from the obvious fact that the authors would like to encourage adults to learn as if they were children. Since the nature of learning differs drastically for the young and for the adults, playing the simplicity of the child learning up and downplaying its pitfalls could give a false impression of ease and provide the adult learners with unrealistic expectations.

Most of the authors' points could be summarized simply as - everything is new for children. This fact alone, could be responsible for enormous motivation, unwavering persistence,  and seemingly endless enthusiasm children display in their learning. Most of the adults loose this sense of novelty native to children; but more pressingly, adults require learning of another quality. One of the reasons children excel at early stages of their development is their ability to mimic, which is also mentioned by the authors. Adults on the other hand are different - having learned the basics, they are bound to develop their own unique writing voice and style. They are in charge of directing their own development as writers as opposed to copying it from others.

There is one salient feature the young and the adult successful learners do share. Most of them, at some point, require the presence of a strong directing influence on their learning path. For the children, it is usually a teacher, parent, or mentor. The adults could could even be directed by a writer they have never met or even a book. The authors, sadly, omit this part. But as one of the authors brings up a personal example of their daughter in the section, I could contrast it with multiple encounters with the children who were neglected in their education both by their school teachers and parents to the point of virtually being illiterate. Did they learn as children do? Undoubtedly, but what exactly did they learn is another question. The outcomes were completely opposite from what the authors are trying to present.

Can adults do all of these: learn as children, protect themselves from disruptive influences, and self-direct towards their own objectives and ideals? Probably not. But they could, no doubt, take some elements of each aspect and make the best of their learning adventure.

* Rawlins, J., & Metzger, S. (2016). The Writer's Way, Ninth Edition. Wadsworth Publishing
